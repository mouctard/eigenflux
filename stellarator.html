How this works

Stellarators confine plasma with a fully three-dimensional, non-axisymmetric magnetic field — there's no 2D poloidal cross-section to reduce the problem to, unlike the tokamak page. Solving a 3D MHD equilibrium (with a real solver like DESC or VMEC) is genuinely heavy — not something to run fresh in a browser tab.

Where this data comes from

These flux surfaces are not solved live. They're read from precomputed equilibria published in the DESC repository itself — real DESC-solved solutions for real stellarator configurations, exported offline (tools/export_stellarators.py) to a compact binary format this page just reads and renders. Four nested flux surfaces per configuration (at normalized radius ρ = 0.3, 0.55, 0.8, 1.0) are shown, colored by nesting depth with the same colormap as the tokamak page.

The configurations

  • Wendelstein 7-X — the operating superconducting stellarator at Max Planck IPP in Greifswald, Germany. 5 field periods, optimized for quasi-isodynamic confinement.
  • HSX — the Helically Symmetric Experiment at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. 4 field periods, the first stellarator built around quasi-helical symmetry.
  • NCSX — the National Compact Stellarator Experiment design from PPPL. 3 field periods, quasi-axisymmetric.

Units and caveats

Coordinates are in meters, at each device's actual real-world scale (W7-X's major radius is several times HSX's, and the two look it here). This page shows geometry — flux-surface shape — not the pressure/current profiles or stability properties those equilibria also contain; a future pass could add those as an overlay.

Live fuel burn, driven by a real plasma volume

Unlike the tokamak page (whose fixed-boundary solve gives no free-boundary plasma volume of its own), this page's flux surfaces already are a real solved 3D boundary, so a real enclosed volume can be computed directly: for each configuration's outermost surface, V = (1/6)|Σ v₀·(v₁×v₂)| summed over the same triangles the viewer already renders — the divergence theorem applied to a closed mesh, the general 3D analog of the tokamak page's Pappus's-theorem volume. Checked against a synthetic torus of exactly known volume before use (tools/validate_stellarator_volume.mjs, converges cleanly, sub-1% error at the resolution actually shipped), and independently against a real published figure: this computation gives 27.9 m³ for W7-X's boundary, against 30 m³ published for the real device — a solid match for an independent geometric calculation from a different equilibrium variant.

From there it's the exact same 0D burn model as the tokamak page (same fuel presets, same energy-capture presets, same closed-form deplete/sustained math) — see its "Choosing a fuel" and "Where the energy goes" panels for the physics. Temperature and density are still illustrative reactor-ballpark numbers (matching the tokamak page's own operating points), not these experiments' real (far colder and thinner) operating conditions — W7-X, HSX, and NCSX are research devices studying confinement physics, not power reactors, so pairing their real geometry with reactor-scale conditions is a what-if extrapolation, the same one the tokamak page already makes.

Play also means the same thing here as on the tokamak page: it energizes the confining field, charging a one-time activation-energy cost (ITER's real 51 GJ magnet-system figure) against cumulative electric output on the energy-vs-time chart — not just a comparison line. Reset de-energizes the field and ends the shot. The flux surfaces fade toward the background as fuel depletes, the same presentation cue (tied to n(t)/n₀, not a re-solved equilibrium) as the tokamak page's 2D and 3D views.

W_th and τ_E: why there's no sawtooth here

Wth = 3·n·T·V and τE = Wth/Ploss use a minimal power balance: an illustrative total input-heating figure (this page has no Bt/Ip/heating "Operating point" preset to derive one from the way the tokamak page does — see ILLUSTRATIVE_OPERATING_POINT.P_heat_MW in src/stellarator/presets.js) minus the same Bremsstrahlung radiated-power estimate the tokamak page uses, at steady state (this page's T/n don't ramp the way the tokamak page's do, so dW/dt = 0). τE is shown as a plain diagnostic number only.

It's deliberately not used to drive a core-brightness throb the way the tokamak page's τE drives a real sawtooth relaxation oscillation there. Checked before assuming otherwise: a tokamak sawtooth is a specific instability (the m/n=1/1 internal kink) tied to a q=1 rational surface created by a net toroidal current profile. W7-X's vacuum rotational-transform profile is flat and doesn't cross a low-order rational surface at all — sawtooth-like crashes have only been produced there deliberately, with off-axis electron-cyclotron current drive pushing the profile through one, which is not this page's modeled scenario. Applying the tokamak's sawtooth here would be a physically dishonest transplant, not just an illustrative simplification, so the core glow instead tracks the real P(t)/P0 directly with no oscillation layered on top. (Edge-localized modes — the tokamak page's other real relaxation oscillation, now driving its D-α shot-trace spike — do have real stellarator precedent, e.g. W7-AS's H-mode ELMs, but ELMs are an edge phenomenon, not a core one, so they don't belong on this light either.)

Neoclassical ripple transport losses (the checkbox)

A tokamak's field is axisymmetric: |B| varies with major radius but not with toroidal angle, so a trapped ("banana-orbit") particle's guiding center drifts stay bounded and the orbit closes on itself. A stellarator's field also varies helically along each flux surface — real, physical ripple, not a numerical artifact — so trapped particles instead follow more complex drift orbits that, in general, don't close. Coulomb collisions continuously (and randomly) kick a particle's pitch angle; occasionally that kick is enough to put a particle onto one of these open drift orbits, carrying it to the wall before it thermalizes. This is neoclassical transport, and in the long-mean-free-path ("1/ν") collisionality regime relevant at reactor temperatures — hot plasma means infrequent collisions — it scales unfavorably as collisionality drops, exactly the wrong direction for a power plant. It's a real, major-enough problem that all three configurations on this page exist specifically to fight it: W7-X was numerically optimized for quasi-isodynamic confinement, HSX for quasi-helical symmetry, and NCSX for quasi-axisymmetry — different routes to the same goal, a magnetic-field-strength symmetry that makes trapped-particle orbits close again the way a tokamak's do.

Checking the box models this as what it actually is — a real random process, not a periodic one. Loss "events" arrive as a Poisson process (exponentially-distributed random real-time intervals, via Math.random(), not a periodic function of time), each briefly derating displayed power and energy by a random fraction before recovering, and briefly dimming the outermost flux surface in the 3D view. The event rate and per-event loss fraction (see src/fusion/neoclassicalLoss.js) are illustrative: the literature is unambiguous that this channel is real and can be severe, but doesn't offer one trustworthy number to calibrate a 0D model against, let alone a per-device split between these three specific configurations — so the same range is used for all three here rather than fabricate precision the physics doesn't actually support. Unchecked (the default) matches every other stat on this page: the idealized case, with this loss channel not modeled.
