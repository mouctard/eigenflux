Follow a single reaction

One D-T reaction, particle by particle, from collision to electricity.

Everything on the main page — fusion power, electric power, the energy chart — comes from averaging over an enormous number of reactions like this one happening every second. This page follows just one, end to end, using the same real numbers (masses, energies, efficiencies) computed elsewhere in this app. The animations are schematic toy models, not simulated particle trajectories — real fusion plasmas don't look like this — but every number attached to them is real, and each step says which kind of approximation it's making.

1. Getting close enough to react

Both particles carry positive charge, so they repel each other electrostatically — the Coulomb barrier. At the temperatures this page's steady-state presets actually use (millions of degrees), the particles' typical kinetic energy is still usually below that barrier classically. The reaction goes ahead anyway because of quantum tunneling: there's a small but non-zero chance of "appearing" on the other side of the barrier without classically having enough energy to climb it. This is exactly the same tunneling factor already inside this project's reactivity formula (see the main page's "Choosing a fuel" panel) — the exp(-3ξ) term in the Bosch-Hale parametrization is the tunneling probability, falling off sharply at low temperature, which is the real reason fusion needs the plasma this hot in the first place.

2. Collision and transformation


    

      The two nuclei fuse into a new, unstable combined state, which immediately breaks apart
      into two different, lighter particles. Add up the masses on each side and they don't
      quite balance — the products together weigh very slightly less than the reactants did.
      That missing mass Δm didn't vanish; Einstein's E = Δmc² says it
      became energy, released as the kinetic energy the two new particles fly apart with. This

3. Why the energy splits unevenly

    
    

    

      The reacting particles were slow (thermal speeds, not the fast products about to be
      created), so to good approximation the two products fly apart with equal and opposite
      momentum, p. Kinetic energy is KE = p²/(2m) — same momentum,
      smaller mass means more kinetic energy. So the lighter product always gets the
      lion's share. Using just the particles' mass numbers as a stand-in for their
      real mass (a toy model, but a good one — see the code comment in
      src/story/reactionStory.js for how closely it matches the real, measured
      split) reproduces  almost exactly.

5. What happens to the neutron

    
    

      The neutron travels in a straight line until it slams into the surrounding blanket
      structure (lithium and beryllium, in real conceptual designs) and is absorbed. Beryllium
      can undergo an (n,2n) reaction — one neutron in, two out — releasing extra
      energy and multiplying the neutron's usable heat by roughly
       (the same blanket-multiplier
      figure the main page's "Blanket + steam turbine" energy-capture option uses). Some of
      those neutrons are captured by lithium instead, via n + ⁶Li → T + ⁴He —
      breeding new tritium fuel. Real D-T reactors need this: tritium is barely present in
      nature, so the reactor has to make its own as it runs.
