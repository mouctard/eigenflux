FAQ ▾

What does a fusion plant operator actually do — and how do I simulate that here?

A shift roughly breaks into five real jobs, each mapped to a control on this page:

1. Configure the scenario. Real operators pick a plasma shape, pressure/current profile, and fuel before a shot. Here: the Shape, Steady state, and Fuel presets in the sidebar — try switching between them and watch the 2D slice and diagnostics badges change.

2. Set the operating point. Real operators choose target field, current, and heating power for the shot. Here: the Operating point preset (JET-scale / DIII-D-scale) sets Bt, Ip, and the auxiliary heating powers everything else derives from.

3. Ignite and ramp. Real operators energize the magnets and ramp up current and heating together. Here: Play — watch the whole dashboard visibly rise over the ~1.4s ramp (slow the speed selector to 0.1×–0.5× to see it in detail).

4. Monitor against limits. This is most of a real shift: watching βN against the Troyon stability limit, q95 against the disruption boundary, H98 against the confinement baseline, and divertor heat flux against engineering limits — all shown live here with their real target ranges (hover the βN/q95 badges, or see the H98 and divertor tiles' target subtext).

5. End the shot. Real operators de-energize the field and log the shot. Here: Reset — ramps back down and zeros the clock, so the next Play starts a fresh shift.

Every one of these is genuinely clickable, not decorative — try changing the Operating point while a shot is playing, or switching Shape/Steady state between shots, and watch how it ripples through the live numbers rather than just reading about it.

How much electricity does an average U.S. home use in a year?

About 10,500 kWh/year (roughly 900 kWh/month), per the U.S. Energy Information Administration's residential survey data — the reference figure this page uses below.

How many homes could this plant's current output power?

At the current electric power reading, sustaining that rate for a full year would cover about — average U.S. homes. Press Play to see this update live — it's computed directly from the same electric-power number in the dashboard, not a separate estimate.

How does that compare to a real power plant?

A full-scale fusion pilot plant is generally targeted in the hundreds of megawatts of net electric output — enough for a few hundred thousand homes continuously. This page's plasma volume and operating points are illustrative and much smaller in scale than that; treat the "homes powered" figure as a unit-conversion demonstration, not a plant design.

What's missing, physics-wise, before "net energy out" here means grid-usable power?

Several real gaps, roughly in order of how much they matter:

1. "Net" here is a much easier bar than real grid net-positive. The "net (out − activation)" stat tile only nets cumulative electric output against a one-time magnet-activation cost. A real plant needs continuously positive power balance against all recirculating loads — auxiliary heating power draw, cryogenic refrigeration for superconducting magnets, tritium processing, cooling pumps — sustained indefinitely, not just paid back once. That's the real distinction between engineering gain Qeng and the plasma-only power balance shown here (see "Live fuel burn" below).

2. Fuel-cycle closure isn't modeled. D-T fusion consumes tritium, which isn't naturally abundant — a real plant's blanket has to breed more tritium than it burns (tritium breeding ratio > 1). That's an unsolved materials/engineering problem this page doesn't model at all.

3. Sustained materials survivability is a real, unsolved problem. The Divertor Ts figure here can exceed tungsten's melting point on a long pulse — try it. That's not a bug; it's an honest preview of the real challenge of divertor materials surviving continuous (not single-shot) operation at grid-relevant duty cycles for years, which no real device has yet demonstrated.

4. Duty cycle. A conventional tokamak's plasma current is driven inductively off the central solenoid, which has a finite flux swing — without steady-state current drive (itself a real recirculating power cost), a shot is inherently pulsed, complicating continuous grid supply.

5. Scale. Hitting a real few-hundred-MW net electric output needs a much larger machine than this page's illustrative plasma volume and operating points — see "How does that compare to a real power plant?" above.

Is fusion the same as the nuclear power in use today?

No — today's nuclear plants use fission (splitting heavy uranium/plutonium nuclei). This page simulates fusion (light hydrogen isotopes combining), which has no chain-reaction meltdown risk (the reaction simply stops if confinement is lost) and produces no long-lived high-level waste, though reactor-wall materials still become activated by neutrons and need handling.

When will real fusion power plants exist?

Genuinely uncertain — it's an active, unsolved engineering challenge, not just a solved-in-principle one. ITER (an international experimental reactor) targets producing net fusion power in the 2030s; several private companies target pilot electricity-producing plants around the same decade. Treat any specific date, including those, skeptically.

Is this page a real simulation, or just an animation?

A real (if simplified) simulation — an actual Grad–Shafranov PDE is solved live for the plasma shape, and the formulas behind most of the dashboard are cited, real physics. See "How this works" below, especially "Approximations vs. reality," for exactly what's rigorously computed versus stylized.

Reactor

Your shift starts now. Hit Play to ignite today's plasma, then watch the dashboard to the right to keep it in optimal operating conditions.

ℹ How Play/Reset/Deplete/Sustained work

Slow down (0.1×–0.5×) the speed to focus on the ignition ramp itself — the critical point when the magnetic field is turned on and the deuterium and tritium are being accelerated. With the B field, current, and heating powers rising together — the gases ignite and turn into a fiery plasma. See "The shot" below.

Pressing Play energizes the confining magnetic field (real reference cost: ITER's 51 GJ magnet system, charged once per shot) and starts accelerating/heating the fuel ions — the field, current, and heating powers ramp up over about 1.4s. The Reset button de-energizes the field (ramping back down) and ends the shot — fuel, clock, and the activation cost all return to zero. The Deplete option burns fuel down following n(t) = n₀/(1 + n₀⟨σv⟩t) — real depletion timescales are tens of seconds to minutes at 1×, so try a higher speed to see it happen quickly. The Sustained option refuels exactly as fast as fuel burns by pumping in the reactants into the chamber, holding the plasma density constant. The chamber sits dark until Play ignites it, brightening to a white-hot core with purple plasma further out as the field ramps up; from there the core throbs on a real sawtooth relaxation oscillation (see "Units" below) and the whole plasma visibly fades as fuel depletes. The wonder of instantaneous fusion power — both above and in the 3D view below, are all represented by the constants and variables in this page.

Operating point

ℹ How Operating point works

The real-unit toroidal field, plasma current, and auxiliary heating actuators can't be derived from the solver's own arbitrary-unit profile amplitudes, so these are new real-unit inputs that a preset selects. These values represent the same burn model's existing temperature/density operating points as follows below. JET-scale is a bigger, higher-field, higher-current machine (real JET: up to 3.45T / 4.8MA); DIII-D-scale is smaller and lower-field (real DIII-D: up to 2.2T / 2MA) but carries real electron-cyclotron heating, which JET's real heating mix never had. See "Plasma diagnostics" below for what that difference actually does to confinement.

Shape

Press Enter to solve. Functions: sin cos tan sqrt abs exp, constant pi.
ℹ How Shape works

Sets the plasma boundary via the standard Miller parametrization: R₀/a (major/minor radius), κ (elongation, height ÷ width), δ (triangularity, D-shaping). Circular has no elongation or triangularity (κ=1, δ=0); ITER-like uses real ITER-like values (κ=1.7, δ=0.33) — more elongated, triangular plasmas sustain more current and confine better, at the cost of needing active vertical-position control (elongated plasmas are vertically unstable); Spherical tokamak is a low-aspect-ratio (R₀/a≈1.6), highly elongated (κ=2.0) shape, the actual design regime real spherical tokamaks (NSTX, MAST) use. Custom takes any r(θ) polar equation. Shape is independent of the real-unit Operating point above — it only sets geometry, which li, βN, and q95 all depend on.

Steady state

ℹ How Steady state works

Picks the pressure/current profile shape the solve uses: p'(ψ)=p₀·(1−ψN)^α, FF'(ψ)=F₀·(1−ψN)^α (p₀/F₀ are solver-internal arbitrary units, not real pascals/amperes — see "Units" below). Low β / High β vary the pressure amplitude p₀ relative to F₀ — a real, qualitative analog of β (plasma pressure ÷ magnetic pressure, see the Variables glossary), though not itself a calibrated real β value; βN in the dashboard is the real, calibrated one. Peaked current / Broad current instead vary the taper exponent α: peaked falls off faster from the core (current concentrated toward the axis, generally higher li, more sawtooth-prone); broad is flatter (more current toward the edge, lower li, generally more stable). Switch between them and watch li respond.

Fuel

ℹ How Fuel works

Picks the fusion reaction: D-T (deuterium-tritium, Q=17.6 MeV, the easiest reaction to ignite and the near-term reactor choice); D-D (deuterium-deuterium, a self-reaction with two ~50/50 branches, Q=4.03/3.27 MeV); D-³He (deuterium-helium-3, Q=18.3 MeV, aneutronic in its primary channel, but needs a much higher Coulomb barrier crossed). Temperature/density stay fixed across fuel choice, so D-³He shows a correctly, physically tiny reaction rate at these D-T-range conditions — not a bug, the actual reason D-T is fusion's near-term fuel of choice. See "Choosing a fuel" below for the reactivity formulas and how they're validated.

Energy capture

ℹ How Energy capture works

Picks how raw fusion power becomes electricity: Blanket + steam turbine multiplies neutron energy in a Li/Be breeder blanket (~1.15×) then runs both neutron and charged-particle heat through a steam cycle (~34% efficiency) — captures everything, at moderate efficiency; Direct conversion electrostatically captures only charged fusion products, at higher efficiency (~60%), but zero neutron energy — good for aneutronic fuels, bad for neutron-heavy ones. Try pairing D-T with Direct conversion, or D-³He with Blanket + steam turbine, to see why the wrong pairing leaves most of the power on the table.

How this works

A tokamak's magnetically confined plasma settles into a steady axisymmetric state described by the Grad–Shafranov equation. This page solves it directly, the way eigendrum solves the clamped-membrane eigenvalue problem for a drum shape — a real PDE, solved live with finite elements, not a canned animation.

The equation

∫∫ (1/R) ∇ψ·∇v  dR dZ  =  ∫∫ ( R p'(ψ) + FF'(ψ)/R ) v  dR dZ,      ψ = 0 on the boundary

ψ is the poloidal flux; its contours are the nested flux surfaces the plasma actually organizes itself along — the direct analog of eigendrum's nodal lines. p'(ψ) and FF'(ψ) are the pressure and toroidal-field profile functions that define a given steady state.

Fixed boundary, on purpose

The plasma boundary shape (elongation, triangularity) is imposed directly here, not computed self-consistently from external coil currents the way a real free-boundary equilibrium code (EFIT, CHEASE) would. That's the direct equivalent of eigendrum's clamped ψ = 0 edge condition, and it's the standard simplification used by fixed-boundary equilibrium solvers.

Custom shapes, by equation

Besides the three fixed presets, the boundary can also be typed directly as a polar equation — the direct analog of eigendrum's "from an equation" panel. The boundary is R(θ) = R₀ + r(θ)cos θ, Z(θ) = r(θ)sin θ for a user-supplied r(θ) (fixed center R₀ = 3.0, matching the circular preset). This needed no new mesher: the O-grid mesh already had a generic path, buildOGridMeshFromBoundary, that linearly interpolates any star-shaped boundary curve in from the center — until now it was only used internally to validate the solver against Solov'ev's own zero-flux contour (see Validation, above). A hand-written expression parser (no eval, matching the "no dependencies" rule below) compiles r(θ) client-side; it's rejected before meshing if it isn't finite and positive everywhere, which would otherwise self-intersect the mesh. Free-hand drawing (eigendrum's other input mode) is deliberately not offered here — a hand-drawn curve is routinely not star-shaped, which would need real unstructured meshing with point-in-polygon repair, for comparatively little physical payoff (a doodle isn't a meaningful plasma boundary the way it's a meaningful drum).

The 3D view

Tokamak equilibria are axisymmetric by construction — nothing depends on the toroidal angle φ — so the 3D torus is literally the 2D (R,Z) solve above, swept through φ ∈ [0, 2π): X = R cos φ, Y = Z, Z = R sin φ. The nested surfaces shown are the O-grid mesh's own ρ-rings (at ρ = 0.15, 0.4, 0.7, 1.0), revolved this way and colored with the same edge-to-core colormap as the 2D plot — the same technique the 3D stellarator viewer uses for its (non-axisymmetric, precomputed) surfaces, just built from this page's own live solve instead of a DESC file. The magnet coils and control cabinets are schematic: sized from the current shape's own bounding box plus a fixed margin, not a specific reactor's engineering design. While Play is running, the core surface's brightness and a point light at the magnetic axis pulse with the same powerLevel/pulse phase already driving the 2D glow (see Live fuel burn, below) — same validated number, two views.

Why no eigensolver

Unlike the drum problem, this isn't an eigenvalue problem — it's a sequence of linear solves. p'(ψ) and FF'(ψ) depend on the unknown ψ itself, so the equation is nonlinear; it's solved by Picard (fixed-point) iteration: guess ψ, normalize it against the current magnetic-axis value, evaluate the profile functions, solve the resulting linear system, repeat until it stops changing. The stiffness matrix depends only on mesh geometry (not on ψ), so it's built once and reused every iteration — only the right-hand side changes — which is why a plain Jacobi-preconditioned conjugate gradient solver is enough, no direct factorization or eigensolver required.

Validation

For the special case where p' and FF' are both constant, a closed-form solution exists — the classical Solov'ev equilibrium (Solov'ev, 1968; see also Freidberg, Ideal Magnetohydrodynamics). This is the same role eigendrum's Bessel-function validation plays for the circular drum. Meshing the solver's mesh against that solution's own zero-flux contour and refining resolution gives clean second-order convergence: 

nRho   nNodes   L2 error
 8      385     6.3e-3
16     1537     1.8e-3   (3.6× reduction)
32     6145     4.8e-4   (3.6× reduction)
64    24577     1.3e-4   (3.7× reduction)

This validation is restricted to up-down symmetric (zero-triangularity) boundaries, which is what the closed form covers exactly. The shaped presets above (ITER-like, spherical tokamak) use the same validated solver and assembly code, just without an independent closed-form check of their own.

Units

Profile amplitudes (p₀, F₀ per steady-state preset) are solver-internal arbitrary units, chosen to make each preset's qualitative behavior clearly visible — not calibrated to real pressures or currents in pascals or amperes.

The canvas legend labels this same solved profile "plasma temperature" rather than "pressure": pressure p = n·k·T, and this page already treats density as spatially uniform everywhere else (the burn model's n₀ is a single scalar, not a profile) — under that assumption p(ψ) ∝ T(ψ) exactly, so the solved pressure shape doubles honestly as a normalized temperature shape T(ψ)/T₀, hottest at the core and coolest at the edge, matching how a real device's core is actually hottest.

Live fuel burn

Press Play to run a real burn calculation on top of the solved equilibrium: the standard 0D power-balance model used for reactor scoping (the same category of calculation behind the Lawson criterion), not a scripted animation. Plasma volume is computed from the actual solved mesh (Pappus's theorem per triangle, treating R,Z as meters). Play doesn't just start a clock — it represents energizing the confining magnetic field, which has a real energy cost: the first Play of a "shot" charges a one-time activation energy (ITER's real 51 GJ magnet-system figure), tracked against cumulative electric output as a real input/output balance — the "net (out − activation)" stat tile above (see "Where the energy goes" for how that conversion is computed), not just a comparison number. Pausing doesn't re-charge it; only Reset — de-energizing the field and ending the shot — does, so the next Play pays it again. Deplete mode has no refueling — dn/dt = -<σv>n² — which has an exact closed-form solution n(t) = n₀/(1+n₀<σv>t), so power and cumulative energy are evaluated directly from elapsed time with no per-frame integration to accumulate error. Sustained mode refuels exactly as fast as fuel burns, holding density (and power) constant — real reactors need continuous pellet injection for exactly this reason, since natural burn-down timescales at these densities are tens of seconds to minutes at 1×, not hours (try a higher speed multiplier to see a full burn-down in a few seconds).

The plasma's fill fades toward the background as fuel depletes (both here and in the 3D view below) — a direct visual read on n(t)/n₀, so a spent plasma no longer looks identical to a fresh one. This is a presentation cue layered on top of the already-solved static equilibrium, not a re-solved Grad-Shafranov shape — the flux-surface contours and boundary stay exactly as solved; only the pressure fill's opacity changes. The 3D magnet coils likewise dim when the field is off (Reset, or before the first Play) and take on a blue energized tint that tracks the same ~1.4s-up/ 1.1s-down ramp as the Bt/Ip/heating diagnostics below, rather than switching instantly.

That same ramp gates the 2D and 3D plasma color itself: the chamber renders unlit (background-colored, though the solved boundary and flux-surface contours stay visible throughout, since the equilibrium math doesn't depend on whether a shot is running) until Play energizes the field, then fades up to the full temperature colormap as the ramp completes, and fades back down on Reset — there's no hot plasma to show without a confining field actually on. On top of that, the core additionally brightens and dims on a real sawtooth relaxation oscillation: repeated partial magnetic reconnection at the q=1 surface (the m/n=1/1 internal-kink instability, Kadomtsev 1975) slowly reheats the core, then rapidly collapses it, over and over — a genuine, named, asymmetric tokamak phenomenon (not a smooth decorative pulse), timed off this page's own live τE diagnostic (period ≈ 1.5×τE, crash depth ≈ 25%, both from published sawtooth-period/τE and crash-amplitude figures — see src/fusion/sawtooth.js for the citations; the crash's on-screen duration is stretched well past its real ~100 µs so it's visible at real-time frame rates).

Temperature and initial fuel density are illustrative, reactor-ballpark operating points assigned per steady-state preset (roughly 6–15 keV, ~0.6–1.1×10²⁰ m⁻³ — in ITER's target range), not derived from the solver's arbitrary-unit pressure profile amplitudes, and the same for every fuel (see below); the equilibrium shape itself stays the already-solved static one rather than being re-solved as fuel depletes. The net-energy figure nets electric output against the one-time magnet-activation cost only — it's still not a true Q-factor, since continuous heating power and energy-confinement-time losses (which a real reactor also has to pay for, continuously, not just once at startup) aren't modeled here; that would need transport/confinement-time physics this page doesn't attempt.

Choosing a fuel

The Fuel selector switches which reaction <σv>(T) and energy release Q feed the burn model above — same 0D power balance, different nuclear physics. All three use the Bosch & Hale (1992) R-matrix parametrization already cited for D-T, extended to D-D's two channels and D-³He: 

D-T:   D + T   → ⁴He(3.5 MeV) + n(14.1 MeV)          Q = 17.6 MeV
D-D:   D + D   → T(1.0 MeV) + p(3.0 MeV)             Q = 4.03 MeV   (~50%)
       D + D   → ³He(0.8 MeV) + n(2.4 MeV)            Q = 3.27 MeV   (~50%)
D-³He: D + ³He → ⁴He(3.6 MeV) + p(14.7 MeV)           Q = 18.3 MeV   (aneutronic)

D-D is a self-reaction (both reactants are deuterium), which changes the bookkeeping but not the burn-down formula above: the reaction-rate pair-counting factor (½, since D-D collisions are counted once per pair) and the fact that each reaction consumes two D ions exactly cancel, so dn/dt = -<σv>n² holds for D-D too, with <σv> summed over both channels — the same closed form, not a coincidence, and checked by re-deriving it before implementing (see src/fusion/burn.js). Power still needs the two D-D channels' different Q weighted separately, since they release different energy and one is neutron-heavy and the other isn't.

All four reaction channels' coefficients were checked to within 0.4% against an independently reproduced copy of the Bosch-Hale paper's own reference table before being used here (tools/validate_reactivity.mjs) — see References. Temperature and density stay fixed across fuel choice (above), so switching to D-³He at these D-T-range temperatures shows a genuinely, correctly tiny reaction rate: D-³He has a higher Coulomb barrier (helium's +2 charge vs. deuterium/tritium's +1) and its reactivity only becomes comparable to D-T's at much higher temperature — real D-³He reactor concepts are generally envisioned at 50–100+ keV, not the 6–15 keV used here. That's not a bug; it's the actual reason D-T is the near-term fusion fuel of choice.

Where the energy goes

Raw fusion power isn't electricity. The Energy capture selector applies a simple two-stage conversion on top of the already-computed power/energy above, split by how much of each fuel's output is carried by neutrons vs. charged particles (from the table above — e.g. D-T is ~80% neutron, D-³He's primary channel is ~0%): 

P_electric = ( n_frac × captures_n × blanket_multiplier  +  c_frac × captures_c ) × η_thermal × P_fusion

Blanket + steam turbine captures both: a Li/Be breeder blanket multiplies neutron energy (~1.15×, typical cited range 1.1–1.3× for conceptual designs — the (n,2n) reactions that also breed tritium release extra energy), then everything drives a steam (Rankine) cycle at ~34% thermal-to-electric efficiency (typical cited range 30–40%). Direct conversion electrostatically decelerates charged fusion products directly into current at a higher ~60% efficiency (theoretical range up to 60–90%, experimental only, never deployed at reactor scale) — but captures none of the neutron energy at all, which is why it does badly on D-T (mostly neutrons) and well on D-³He (no neutrons in the primary channel). Try pairing them: D-T + blanket/steam is the closest to a real near-term reactor; D-³He + direct conversion is the "no neutrons anywhere" aspirational case; the other two pairings show why picking the wrong capture technology for a fuel leaves most of the power on the table. These multiplier/efficiency numbers are illustrative literature ballparks for conceptual designs, the same epistemic status as the temperature/density operating points above — not a specific engineering design, and not independently closed-form-checked the way the solver or the reactivities are.

The "net (out − activation)" stat tile is this electric-output figure (not raw fusion energy) minus whatever's actually been charged so far — 0 before the first Play, the magnet's activation energy afterward, per the "Live fuel burn" section above. It starts negative right after Play (the activation cost is paid up front) and climbs as electric energy accumulates; the moment it crosses zero is the real breakeven point — cumulative electric output has paid back the one-time activation cost — at whatever fuel/capture settings are currently active.

Plasma diagnostics

Near the 2D slice and in the "Plasma parameters" panel below, a set of real JET/DIII-D- style operational numbers are shown — Bt, Ip, an H-mode/L-mode badge, βN, q95, Wth, H98, τE, li. These sit on top of the arbitrary-unit solve above via a new Operating point preset (real Tesla/MA/MW inputs — Bt, Ip, and auxiliary heating powers — which can't be derived from the solver's own profile amplitudes, so they're new inputs, same epistemic tier as the burn model's existing temperature/density operating points). From there, most of what's shown is genuinely computed, not hand-set:

JET-scale vs. DIII-D-scale, concretely: JET-scale reproduces real JET's field/current envelope (up to 3.45T / 4.8MA — the largest conventional tokamak ever built, and the only one besides TFTR to have actually run D-T fuel before ITER); DIII-D-scale reproduces real DIII-D's (up to 2.2T / 2MA — a smaller, more agile research device at General Atomics built for studying plasma shaping and real-time control, not raw fusion output). That's not just a magnitude difference: JET-scale's higher Bt and Ip feed directly into IPB98(y,2)'s Ip0.93·Bt0.15 dependence above, so at otherwise-equal density and heating power it predicts meaningfully longer τE than DIII-D-scale — the same physical reason bigger, higher-current real devices confine better, and why ITER (bigger and higher-current still) is expected to confine better yet. The two presets also carry a real heating-technology difference, not just scale: JET-scale has zero PECH (real JET's heating mix was neutral beams and ion- cyclotron only — it never had an electron-cyclotron system), while DIII-D-scale includes real PECH (DIII-D has a versatile, high-power ECH system, used in practice for real-time control of tearing modes and current profiles). One thing the Operating point preset does not set: plasma geometry (major/minor radius, elongation) — that's the separate Shape preset above, so pairing JET-scale field/current with, say, the spherical-tokamak shape is a genuine what-if combination, not a real device replica.

  • li (internal inductance) = ⟨Bθ²⟩volume / Bθ²(edge), computed directly from the already-solved ψ field: Bθ ∝ |∇ψ|/R, with a constant gradient per triangle from the same P1 shape-function coefficients assembleStiffness already uses. Since Bθ is proportional to ∇ψ everywhere, this ratio is invariant to ψ's arbitrary overall scale — genuinely computable from the arbitrary-unit solve, no calibration needed. Checked against the textbook li=0.5 result for a flat current-density profile (tools/validate_flux_diagnostics.mjs), converging cleanly as mesh resolution increases.
  • Wth (thermal stored energy) = 3·ne·T·V, the kinetic definition (Te=Ti=T, ne≈ni, the same single-temperature assumption the burn model's operating points already make), using the already-computed real plasma volume (Pappus's theorem, see "Live fuel burn") and the operating point's real ne/T — real, no new calibration.
  • βN follows from that same Wth: volume-averaged pressure p̄ = (2/3)(Wth/V), β = p̄/(Bt²/2μ₀), βN = β[%]·a[m]·Bt[T]/Ip[MA] — real, using the new Bt/Ip inputs and the shape's own real minor radius.
  • q95 uses Ampère's law to turn the new Ip input into a real poloidal field at the edge (Bθ(a) = μ₀Ip/2πa), combined with the standard large-aspect-ratio, elongation-corrected safety factor q ≈ (2πa²Bt)/(μ₀R₀Ip) · (1+κ²)/2 (Wesson, Tokamaks). This is an approximation, not the exact flux-surface- averaged line integral at the 95%-flux surface — see "Not in this first pass".
  • τE and H98: τE,actual = Wth/Ploss (the literal definition of energy confinement time, using the power balance below) is compared against the IPB98(y,2) ELMy H-mode scaling law — τE = 0.0562 · Ip^0.93 · Bt^0.15 · n̄e^0.41 · Ploss^-0.69 · R₀^1.97 · κ^0.78 · ε^0.58 · M^0.19 (ITER Physics Basis / ITPA database) — and H98 = τE,actual / τE,IPB98 is a genuinely computed diagnostic ratio, exactly how it's reported for real shots, not a dial the preset sets directly.
  • H-mode / L-mode badge: compares the auxiliary heating power (PNBI+PECH+PICH) against the Martin08 L-H transition threshold scaling, PLH[MW] = 0.0488 · n̄e[10²⁰m⁻³]^0.717 · Bt^0.803 · S[m²]^0.941 · (2/Aeff), using the plasma's real surface area (Pappus's theorem on the boundary polyline, the same technique as the volume calculation, one level down). The threshold number itself (PLH) is shown live in "Power and fusion", next to the auxiliary heating actually being supplied, so you can see how much margin there is above or below the H-mode transition — the closest thing on this page to operator guidance, since it's a real computed limit, not an arbitrary target.
  • Pα reuses the burn model's own already-computed split — chargedFrac × P_fusion(t) — from "Choosing a fuel", not a new estimate.

All the formulas above marked real were verified this session against independently re-transcribed references or hand-worked arithmetic before shipping (tools/validate_diagnostics.mjs, tools/validate_flux_diagnostics.mjs) — see Verification-style checks throughout this panel for the project's general discipline on that.

Power balance

The "Power and fusion" panel is a standard 0D power balance: Pheat = POH + PNBI + PECH + PICH + Pα (the auxiliary-heating inputs from the Operating point preset, plus the reused Pα above); Ploss = Pheat − Prad − dW/dt; Pin = Pheat; Pout = Prad + Ploss + dW/dt. Pout sums to exactly Pin by construction here — a real balance equation, not fitted to match any particular example shot's numbers (which need not balance exactly). dW/dt is a genuine transient: a finite difference of Wth, nonzero during the magnet/current ramp (see "The shot" below) and ~0 at flat-top. Prad is the one input in this balance whose formula is illustrative rather than checked — this session couldn't verify a trustworthy Bremsstrahlung coefficient/unit convention from source, so it uses an ne²√T-shaped functional form calibrated to a representative point. Everything downstream of it (Ploss, τE, H98) inherits that one soft input, same as any real power-balance analysis inherits its radiated-power measurement's own uncertainty.

Divertor

Unlike the earlier version of this section, λq, fdet, and qi are now built from real, named physical effects rather than one fitted constant apiece — simplified and using representative (not this specific device's certified) parameter values, but genuine divertor physics, not placeholders:

  • λq is the Eich et al. (2013 Nucl. Fusion 53, 093031) regression-#14 scaling, λq[mm] = 0.63·Bθ,edge[T]−1.19, evaluated at the Ampère's-law edge field derived above. The exponent (−1.19) is independently cross-checked against the published scaling; the 0.63 coefficient is consistent with an independent ITER-extrapolation check (λq ≲ 1mm at ITER's Bθ ~1T) but wasn't confirmed digit-for-digit against the primary paper this session.
  • qi decomposes the target heat flux into two real, named effects: the parallel SOL power channel of width λq widens poloidally at the target by the poloidal flux expansion fx = Bθ,midplane/Bθ,target (real devices run fx~3–6; 4 used here), then that footprint is stretched further by projection onto a target tilted at a shallow grazing/wetted incidence angle θ (real design values ~2–3°; 2.5° used here) — qi = Ploss / (2 legs · fx/sinθ · 2πR₀λq). These are exactly the two real mechanisms (flux expansion, grazing incidence) that let real divertor targets survive heat loads that would otherwise be unmanageable — using representative published parameter ranges, not this device's own engineering design.
  • fdet = Prad/(Prad+Ploss), the real fraction of exhaust power already radiated away (by seeded or intrinsic impurities) before it would otherwise reach the target — the actual real-world mechanism divertor detachment exploits. A simplification of full 2-point-model SOL physics (which also tracks momentum and pressure loss along the flux tube, not modeled here), but built entirely from this page's own already-real Prad/Ploss, not a separate fitted number.
  • Ts uses real 1D transient conduction into a semi-infinite tungsten solid under constant flux qi (a standard heat-transfer result): ΔT = (2qi/e)·√(t/π), where e = √(kρc) is tungsten's thermal effusivity (k=120 W/m/K, ρ=19300 kg/m³, c=134 J/kg/K — representative elevated- temperature values, not a certified alloy grade) and t is the real elapsed time since this shot's magnets were energized — so Ts genuinely rises the longer a shot runs, exactly like a real target, and does not model cooling between pulses.

What's still not modeled: the self-consistent 2-point SOL model (upstream/target pressure and momentum balance), impurity transport driving the radiation in fdet, and any target-material erosion/damage threshold. Treat the divertor numbers as real simplified physics with representative parameters, not a specific engineered design.

The shot

Press Play and the confining field doesn't just switch on: Ip, Bt, and the auxiliary heating powers ramp up over about 1.4 seconds (real time, independent of the fuel-burn clock's speed multiplier — a magnet/current ramp is a hardware timescale, not a simulated-time one), the 3D coils brighten continuously with it, and every diagnostic above — βN, q95, τE, H98, the power balance, the H-mode gate — recomputes each frame from these ramping values, so the whole dashboard visibly rises together rather than snapping on. Pause or Reset ramps back down over about 1.1 seconds the same way, independently of the fuel-burn clock (which still freezes/zeros exactly as before — see "Live fuel burn"). Slow the burn-speed selector to 0.1×–0.5× to watch the ramp itself in detail rather than blowing past it. At flat-top, a small deterministic texture (a couple of off-frequency sine waves, the same idiom as the 2D glow's pulse) is layered onto Ip, Wth, and a stylized D-α trace with periodic ELM-like spikes, so the "Shot trace" strip chart settles around a fixed value rather than sitting dead flat — an illustrative shot-trace texture, not a turbulence simulation. That texture is deliberately kept out of dW/dt's finite difference (computed from a separate, unjittered trajectory), so the power balance above stays a real ~0 at flat-top instead of amplifying cosmetic noise into a spurious transient. The existing fusion-power/energy stat tiles above (from "Live fuel burn") keep representing that already-validated closed form directly, unramped — the ramp modulates the new real-unit diagnostics layered on top, not the underlying 0D burn physics. The "Shot trace" chart renders in real time frame-by-frame, one push per animation frame, so it's most legible at lower speed multipliers rather than the fast-forward settings.

Not in this first pass

The exact flux-surface-averaged line integral for q(ψ) — and full safety-factor profiles / rational-surface resonances more generally, the direct fusion analog of "striking a resonant mode" — still need contour extraction at arbitrary ψ levels and aren't implemented here; q95 above uses a documented large-aspect-ratio approximation instead (see "Plasma diagnostics"). Stellarators are inherently three-dimensional and aren't handled by this page at all; they're a separate viewer built from equilibria precomputed offline (VMEC/DESC) rather than solved live in-browser — see the 3D stellarator viewer. The burn model doesn't re-solve the equilibrium as pressure drops and doesn't evolve temperature — T/n stay the fixed operating-point values from "Live fuel burn" throughout a shot, even though the new power balance above now does track input heating power and energy confinement time as real quantities. D-³He's real side reactions (it's not perfectly aneutronic in practice — some D-D branching still happens in a D-³He plasma) aren't modeled; only the primary D+³He channel is.
